# Creality CR-PLA: literature baseline for interlayer failure

**Recorded:** 2026-09-25; source check refreshed 2026-09-26

**Baseline material:** Creality CR-PLA (PLA, 1.75 mm), with other Creality products and generic PLA kept as separate comparison records

**Status:** literature reference only; not a qualified material record for this user's spool, K1C, slicer profile, or cohesive-zone model.

### Decision-grade summary

Creality CR-PLA remains the baseline product. The strongest directly relevant public result I found for this exact Creality product is a small, non-peer-reviewed layer-adhesion screen: two reported break loads of 36.20 and 35.95 kg on upright PLA coupons, with a 4 × 4 mm minimum section. If the chart's kg means kgf, these convert to nominal whole-coupon stresses of 22.19 and 22.03 MPa (mean 22.11 MPa). Keep these as **screening observations only**: the source does not provide enough batch traceability, replicate statistics, force curves, or fracture-plane evidence to qualify that number as interface strength. The test page identifies the layer-adhesion test, and the downloadable chart contains the two loads; its specimen-method page describes vertical printing with maximum perimeters. [MyTechFun test overview](https://www.mytechfun.com/video/123), [results chart](https://www.mytechfun.com/download/123/results-creality-filaments.png), [specimen method](https://www.mytechfun.com/pla/).

The Creality-branded CR-PLA TDS copies report Z-direction tensile strengths of 6.63 MPa and 8.63 MPa, respectively, but do not provide enough specimen and failure details to determine that either is a direct interlayer-fracture measurement. The sources conflict, so do not average them or interpret 6.63 MPa as a guaranteed conservative bound. [CR-PLA TDS v2.0 mirror](https://assets.spoolscout.com/tds-sds/creality/tds/creality-cr-pla-tds-v2.0-2021-12.pdf), [conflicting same-name TDS copy](https://www.vital3d.co.uk/_downloads/c987272aabbd74b34e71b1c5035d70ee).

**Baseline value for strength/cohesive calculations: none.** Record the MyTechFun values only as literature observations and keep the TDS values as conflicting manufacturer-document references. I did not find a traceable CR-PLA Mode-I/II crack-growth test or a physical, batch-qualified traction-separation law. A peer-reviewed generic-PLA study does physically isolate interfaces in tensile coupons, but its 33.75 MPa interface strength is inferred by fitting a cohesive FEM model to 22.8 MPa coupon tests; the PLA brand/process is not Creality, so it is method context only. [Perez et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1089/3dp.2020.0109).

**Evidence verdict:** Physical tests on the named Creality CR-PLA exist: Ilić et al. (2024) printed and tensile-tested 27 coupons on a Creality Ender 3 Pro, but varied infill rather than testing layer-normal failure or a crack along an interface. Abebe et al. (2026) also printed Creality CR-PLA and reported tensile coupons plus qualitative porosity/interlayer erosion observations after DSC thermal cycling; these were not layer-normal interface or crack-growth tests. A separate two-specimen, non-peer-reviewed upright layer-adhesion screen is associated with Creality CR-PLA, but its individual coupons are not batch-traceable. Its reported 36.20 and 35.95 kg break loads correspond to 22.19 and 22.03 MPa nominal coupon stress respectively if kg means kgf and the stated minimum 4 × 4 mm cross-section is used (mean: 22.11 MPa). A Creality-branded CR-PLA TDS separately reports 6.63 MPa Z-direction coupon tensile strength; a second same-named TDS copy reports 8.63 MPa. The accessible copies are the [mirrored v2.0 TDS PDF](https://assets.spoolscout.com/tds-sds/creality/tds/creality-cr-pla-tds-v2.0-2021-12.pdf) and [Vital3D's conflicting PDF mirror](https://www.vital3d.co.uk/_downloads/c987272aabbd74b34e71b1c5035d70ee), not traceable Creality-hosted raw test reports. The TDS values lack published specimen details, replicate count, scatter and failure-plane evidence. I found no CR-PLA DCB/ENF/MMB fracture curve or cohesive law. The exact-product coupon data below are useful baseline context, but none support a reliable cohesive calculation. Hyper PLA is a separate Creality product and its flexural delamination observations are kept separate.

The companion MCP resource `plasticity://strength/interlayer-literature-baseline` keeps Creality CR-PLA as the single-material starting reference. The mirrored v2.0 TDS reports 6.63 MPa tensile strength in Z (versus 31 MPa in X-Y), while the Vital3D-hosted copy reports 8.63 MPa in Z (and 48–51 MPa in X-Y). Record both without averaging; this 6.63–8.63 MPa documentary spread is not a statistical interval or conservative design bound. The TDS says values are for reference/comparison and actual printed properties may vary with process. The model assumes one material throughout the part and all its printed layers; do not combine CR-PLA and Hyper PLA data or model dissimilar-material interfaces.

### Baseline decision for CR-PLA

Use Creality CR-PLA as the named reference product, but **do not assign it a numeric interlayer-strength property from the public literature**. The 22.11 MPa derived mean from MyTechFun is the closest public CR-PLA-associated vertical layer-adhesion screen, not a qualified CR-PLA value: retain the individual 22.19 / 22.03 MPa calculations, raw 36.20 / 35.95 kg loads, assumed kgf conversion, stated 4 × 4 mm minimum section, and provenance limitations. The original source reports kg loads and does not publish uncertainty for this conversion. Keep the manufacturer's 6.63 MPa Z value and conflicting 8.63 MPa TDS report as separate documentary evidence. Do not average or choose among these figures as a safe limit. None is a guaranteed lower bound or design allowable, and none goes into the cohesive solver. A coupon peak does not measure crack-growth energy, initial cohesive stiffness, shear response or a traction-separation law.

### Physical tensile tests of the exact Creality CR-PLA product

Ilić et al. physically tested 27 tensile coupons made from 1.75 mm Creality CR-PLA on a Creality Ender 3 Pro. They report Cura's Ender 3 Pro / Generic PLA profile, 210 °C nozzle, 50 °C bed, 60 mm/s, and three coupons per infill condition. Mean tensile strengths were 30.0 MPa (Grid), 30.2 MPa (Lines), 28.8 MPa (Triangle), 30.1 MPa (Cubic), 29.8 MPa (Lighting), 30.6 MPa (Gyroid), and 32.6 MPa (Zig-Zag), all at 60% infill; 46.9 MPa at 100% infill; and 52.9 MPa for 100% wall. Their paper says the test followed ASTM D638, although its preprocessing section describes a specimen as ASTM D790-compliant. It reports means but no tabulated per-coupon results, scatter, or confidence intervals. [Primary paper PDF](https://mma.ftn.uns.ac.rs/papers/MMA-2024-08-003.pdf).

This is useful physical baseline evidence for the exact named filament, but it does not document an upright Z-build coupon or isolate an interlayer interface; the study's variable is infill pattern/density. Do not read its 28.8–52.9 MPa tensile values as interlayer strength, a lower bound, or a cohesive parameter. It used an Ender 3 Pro / Cura process, not the user's K1C process.

The primary paper explicitly identifies 1.75 mm CR-PLA and the Ender 3 Pro test printer, but its specimens are not a physical delamination test: the published comparison varies infill and reports ordinary tensile-coupon failure. Treat this as same-product bulk-coupon context only.

Abebe et al. (2026) identify their blue material as Creality CR-PLA and physically print 4D spiral coupons at five thicknesses; the article does not identify a spool batch. They report tensile tests and post-DSC microscopy; the paper describes rough/porous CR-PLA fracture morphology and interlayer erosion/de-bonding after thermal cycling. This is useful evidence that defects and thermal history affect the same named filament, but the experiment does not isolate loading normal to a printed interface, monitor a delamination crack, or measure `G_Ic`, `G_IIc` or a traction-separation law. The paper's tensile coupon orientation and specimen-level traceability are insufficient to treat its reported tensile results as an interlayer property or user's K1C process data. Keep the qualitative observation only. [Primary open-access article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.polymer.2026.130199).

A separate [MyTechFun Creality filament test](https://www.mytechfun.com/video/123) explicitly includes a layer-adhesion test and identifies CR-PLA among the tested filaments; the author says the PLA filaments in the test were purchased. Its downloadable [results chart](https://www.mytechfun.com/download/123/results-creality-filaments.png) reports two PLA layer-adhesion break loads, 36.20 kg and 35.95 kg, with a displayed mean of 36.08 kg. The chart identifies a minimum cross-section of 4 × 4 mm; the author's [test-method page](https://www.mytechfun.com/pla/) says the layer-adhesion specimen is printed vertically with maximum perimeters. If chart kg denotes kgf, the two nominal coupon stresses are 22.19 and 22.03 MPa (mean 22.11 MPa; each is `load × 9.80665 N/kgf / 16 mm²`). These are calculated average-section stresses, not directly measured interface tractions. The overview and specimen-method pages support classifying this as a CR-PLA-associated screening result, but the chart itself labels only “PLA” and does not trace the tested coupons to a spool/batch. It is the closest publicly available physical layer-normal result found for the named CR-PLA product, not a fracture-mechanics delamination result.

Keep this result strictly as an independent, low-confidence comparison: it is a non-peer-reviewed test, only two specimens are reported, the result table labels the material category “PLA” (the associated review identifies CR-PLA but does not visibly tie each specimen to a batch), test-machine calibration and uncertainty are not published, and the test is not shown as a standard-qualified interface test. The 22.1 MPa is a derived average specimen stress, not a directly measured local interface traction. It is neither a conservative lower bound nor an allowable, and it gives no fracture energy, shear strength, or traction-separation law. Do not enter it into the cohesive solver or combine it with manufacturer TDS values. It can guide an explicitly labeled literature comparison and future test planning only.

## What the physical tests establish

The closest material-specific source is Stanciu et al., *Experimental and Numerical Investigation of Mechanical Properties of Hyper Polylactic Acid (HPLA)*, *Polymers* 18(5), 624 (2026), [doi:10.3390/polym18050624](https://doi.org/10.3390/polym18050624). The authors identify a new/sealed spool of Creality Hyper PLA and print it on a Creality K1 Max. Their flat tensile coupons were tested to ISO 527-2:2025, five samples per raster group; their three-point-bend coupons were tested to ISO 178:2019, six per group. The article reports delamination between printed layers in broken flexural specimens, attributed to shear stress. This is direct physical evidence that interlayer delamination is a relevant failure mode for Creality Hyper PLA.

Reported process: 0.4 mm nozzle; 0.2 mm first layer followed by 0.16 mm layers; 220 °C nozzle; 60 °C bed; 100% infill; two contour walls; 300 mm/s; raster groups concentric, 45°, 90°, and 0/90°. All tensile coupons were printed flat. The tested printer, process and material batch therefore do not match the user's K1C process.

| Physical result | Reported mean / observation | How to use it |
|---|---:|---|
| Flat tensile coupon, concentric raster (HPLA-T0) | 42.13 MPa | In-plane printed-coupon reference only |
| Flat tensile coupon, crossed 0/90 raster | 36.96 MPa | In-plane printed-coupon reference only |
| Flat tensile coupon, 45° raster | 34.20 MPa | In-plane printed-coupon reference only |
| Flat tensile coupon, 90° raster | 28.24 MPa | In-plane printed-coupon reference only |
| Three-point flexure, concentric / 0/90 / 45 / 90 groups | 74 / 64 / 65 / 51 MPa flexural stress; layer delamination was observed in broken sections of the non-concentric groups | Failure-mode and process-sensitivity evidence; these are not interlayer tensile or shear strengths |

The article's flexural stress values are nominal beam stresses. Do not reinterpret them as layer-interface tractions. Its flat tensile tests do not pull normal to the layer planes.

## Direct same-material interlayer fracture tests (generic PLA comparison)

Lambiase et al. physically tested single-material FDM PLA using double-cantilever-beam (DCB) specimens with the crack opened along a printed layer interface. The abstract reports Mode-I critical energy-release rates of 0.75 kJ/m² for monodirectional 0° roads, 2.4 kJ/m² for monodirectional 90° roads, 2.0 kJ/m² for alternating 0°/90°, and 3.9 kJ/m² for alternating ±45°. Its conclusion separately gives about 0.74 kJ/m² for longitudinal 0° roads and about 2.0 kJ/m² for the other monodirectional case. Preserve this 90° summary discrepancy instead of silently selecting one value. Each deposition strategy had five specimens; the build used a 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers and 100% extrusion multiplier, with no exterior shell so the specimen was homogeneous. The paper describes stable crack growth and microscopy of the fracture surfaces, while ±45° specimens showed layer pull-out and potentially mixed-mode behavior. [Primary DCB paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00170-023-12223-1).

For test planning only, the paper's DCB specimen had a reported length of 125 mm, width of 25 mm, initial crack length of 55 mm, and 30 mm from load line to the initial crack tip. Its thickness was selected within 4.5–7.5 mm depending on material/geometry calculations, rather than given as one universal value. The study used five coupons per deposition strategy; that is a study choice, not a prescribed or statistically qualified sample count. Its methods describe ASTM D5528-based design while the conclusion cites D5568, so do not claim formal standard compliance from this paper. The MCP test planner exposes these dimensions as a generic-PLA literature starting reference only; they must be checked for the selected exact-process properties, fixture and documented test method before making coupons.

This is direct physical evidence for interlayer fracture in one PLA material, but the accessible paper does not identify the spool as Creality CR-PLA or Hyper PLA and does not publish specimen-level raw curves here. Raster orientation changes the result substantially. The reported `GIc` values are fracture-energy measurements, not peak tensile/shear traction, a traction-separation curve, `GIIc`, or a Creality/K1C material qualification. The paper identifies its specimen geometry as ASTM D5528-based in the methods but refers to D5568 in its conclusion; preserve that reporting inconsistency too. Treat the reported 0.74/0.75–3.9 kJ/m² values only as literature comparison context, never as a Creality design allowable or a calibrated solver input.

## Values deliberately left unqualified

The Creality Hyper PLA tests above do **not** publish a direct normal-to-layer tensile strength, Mode-I fracture energy `GIc`, Mode-II fracture energy `GIIc`, measured interface traction-separation curve, or process-bound cohesive stiffness. Thus the numerical cohesive law needed for a reliable delamination calculation remains unavailable. Do not enter the reported flat tensile or flexural strengths as cohesive strengths, do not infer `GIc`/`GIIc` from them, and do not use the feedstock filament datasheet strength as printed-layer adhesion.

## Physical Z-direction tensile evidence for comparison

No published Z-normal tensile test was found for the same Creality Hyper PLA spool/product. Two studies on non-Creality-identified PLA provide useful method and magnitude context, but neither qualifies Creality filament:

| Physical result | Reported value | Scope and limitation |
|---|---:|---|
| Eryildiz (2021), [upright D638 Type IV PLA tensile coupon](https://doi.org/10.26701/ems.881254), load normal to build layers | 35.52 MPa nominal specimen tensile strength; the paper reports interlayer fracture and 36% less strength than flat coupons | Commercial PLA of unspecified brand on a Creality Ender 3 Pro; 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layer height, 30% infill, three walls, 200 °C nozzle, 60 °C bed, 60 mm/s; Instron 8872 at 1 mm/min. The paper does not state replicate count or scatter. This is Z-build coupon failure stress, not an isolated interface law. |
| Perez et al. (2021), [custom tensile specimen designed to isolate interfaces](https://doi.org/10.1089/3dp.2020.0109) | 22.8 MPa mean physical printed-specimen tensile strength; 33.75 MPa mean interface strength inferred by calibrating cohesive FEM to the tests | Generic PLA, brand/process not identified in the accessible research record. Fracture was observed to debond at interfaces. The 33.75 MPa is a fitted interface parameter, not a direct physical traction measurement and not transferable to Creality Hyper PLA or CR-PLA. |

These values are external screening context only. The 35.52 MPa value is not a conservative bound and the fitted 33.75 MPa value is not a direct physical measurement of interface traction. They show that an upright tensile coupon can fail at the printed interlayer plane, but they cannot replace testing the user's exact Creality spool and K1C/Creality Print or OrcaSlicer process.

## MCP policy and next qualification step

This baseline is useful to explain expected anisotropy, select test directions and compare future K1C coupons. It does **not** provide a defensible numeric layer-adhesion value for CR-PLA or predict the onset/growth of delamination. It is **not** an exact-process material-coupon record and must not satisfy `plasticity_analyze_cohesive_interface`, static-analysis allowables, or a strength/print-readiness verdict. The MCP must continue to require physical, traceable, exact-process evidence for cohesive analysis.

For the user's chosen single-material process, obtain at minimum:

1. A measured slicer/process identity for Creality CR-PLA on the selected printer and profile (including orientation, infill percentage and pattern, wall loops, top/bottom shell layers, nozzle temperature and actual layer height), plus a Z-normal tensile coupon series with documented failure plane and force/displacement data.
2. For Mode I, a same-process DCB test with crack-growth/energy data; for mixed mode, same-process ENF and at least two MMB conditions, plus measured initial stiffness `K` and a common interface normal/shear direction.
3. Replicates and specimen-level data with dimensions, conditioning, test standard, uncertainty/statistics, and photographs of fracture surfaces. These measured records can then enter the immutable material-interface registry; literature values remain separate.
